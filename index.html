Fundamental concepts of rock mechanics

Module 1 · slides 1–2, 29

This webinar follows Module 1 of the Geomechanica course. It covers how intact rock deforms and fails, how discontinuities control rock mass behaviour, how failure criteria and constitutive models describe that behaviour, and why the in-situ stress field is the starting point for analysing any underground or surface excavation.

The material is written for people who work in mines: geotechnical engineers, mine planners, geologists, ground-control and production staff. Each screen starts with the key idea, then lets you go deeper one layer at a time. You can run it alone at your own pace, or a facilitator can present it on a shared screen.

Learning objectives

By the end of the webinar you should be able to:

  1. Read a complete stress–strain curve from a uniaxial compression test and name the stages of brittle failure.
  2. Explain why and tensile strength are not intrinsic material properties, and how confinement changes the failure mode.
  3. Use the Mohr–Coulomb and Hoek–Brown criteria to describe strength as a function of principal stresses.
  4. Describe the geometric and mechanical properties of , including stiffness and the Barton–Bandis shear strength criterion.
  5. Explain how discontinuities reduce rock mass strength, how the Generalized Hoek–Brown criterion uses , and when that criterion should not be used.
  6. Distinguish linear-elastic and elasto-plastic constitutive models and the post-peak options for different rock mass qualities.
  7. Define the in-situ stress state and explain why it is the most important boundary condition for analysing underground structures.

Section 1 · Intact rock · 1.1

Uniaxial compression behaviour

Module 1 · slide 4

Key concept

A cylindrical core loaded along its axis gives a characteristic stress–strain curve: concave-up, linear, concave-down, peak, then post-peak.

Why it matters

The and from this test are the most common intact-rock inputs to mine design and numerical models.

You should understand

What causes each part of the curve, where E is measured, and that cracking starts well before the peak.

In the uniaxial (unconfined) compression test a rock cylinder is loaded axially with no lateral support. Axial stress is plotted against axial strain. The axial strain is the variable we control and the stress is what we measure. The curve rises to a maximum, the uniaxial compressive strength (UCS), and then falls in the post-peak region.

Move the loading slider or press play to follow the curve and see what is happening inside the sample at each stage.

  • Non-linear, concave upwards at low strain: closure of existing micro-cracks and the initial lack of parallelism between the loading platens and the sample ends.
  • Linear response: the elastic region. Its slope gives Young’s modulus E.
  • Non-linear, concave downwards before the peak: intense micro-cracking reduces the stiffness.
  • Peak: the maximum stress the sample can sustain, the UCS.
  • Post-peak: the sample still carries load, but less as strain increases.

Rock is not a perfect solid: it contains grain boundaries, pores and micro-cracks. Early loading closes cracks that lie roughly perpendicular to the load, so the sample stiffens (concave up). Once they are closed, the grain skeleton deforms elastically and the curve is straight.

Acoustic emission (AE) monitoring, which records the small elastic waves released by cracking, shows that new micro-cracks start at about 0.4 UCS and that failure is a progressive process that continues until the sample is destroyed. The peak is not a single event; it is the point where accumulated cracking can no longer carry additional load.

Supplementary Exploration and geotechnical drill core is routinely tested in uniaxial compression to characterise ore and host rocks. When you read a test report, check that E was taken from the linear part of the curve and not from the initial concave-up part, which would give a value that is too low.

The 0.4 UCS crack-initiation level from the module is a reminder that rock around an opening can be damaged at stresses well below its UCS. Eberhardt et al. (1998) noted that crack initiation and crack damage thresholds are more characteristic of the rock than peak strength, which depends on loading conditions.

Young’s modulus is the slope of the linear part:

E = Δσaxial / Δεaxial (linear region only)

Supplementary Testing laboratories commonly report a tangent modulus at a stated stress level (often 50 % of peak) or an average modulus over the linear portion. Always record which definition was used.

The post-peak part can only be measured in a stiff or servo-controlled machine. In a soft machine, energy stored in the frame is released at the peak and the sample fails violently, so the post-peak branch is lost.

Deeper: why the curve is plotted against strain, not stress

The module’s figure notes that axial strain is the independent variable (“the one we control”) and axial stress is the dependent variable (“the one we measure”). If the test were run by controlling stress, the machine could not follow the descending post-peak branch: once the peak is passed, any further load increase would cause uncontrolled failure. Strain control lets the sample unload gradually and reveals the post-peak behaviour that matters for rock around excavations, where the surrounding rock mass controls the deformation.

Facilitator notes Supplementary

Pause the animation at the start of the linear region and at 0.4 UCS. Ask: “If cracks are already forming at 40 % of the UCS, what does that mean for the rock in a pillar loaded to 60 % of its lab strength?”

Section 1 · Intact rock · 1.2

Brittle failure process

Module 1 · slide 5

Key concept

Brittle failure develops in five stages that can be read from axial, lateral and volumetric strain, and from acoustic emission.

Why it matters

The stage thresholds mark when damage begins and when it becomes unstable, long before the peak.

You should understand

The meaning of crack closure, crack initiation, crack damage and peak strength, and why volumetric strain reverses.

A strain-gauged UCS test records axial strain (along the load) and lateral strain (across it). From these, the is calculated. Together with acoustic emission, the three curves reveal five stages of brittle failure. Play the animation, or click a stage, to see the sample, the curves and the AE record together.

  • I. Micro-crack closure and crushing of asperities (crack closure stress σcc).
  • II. Linear-elastic behaviour.
  • III. Onset of dilation and stable crack propagation, from the crack initiation stress σci (≈ 40 % σf in the figure).
  • IV. Unstable crack growth, from the crack damage stress σcd (≈ 80 % σf), where volumetric strain reverses.
  • V. Sample failure and brittle post-peak.

In stage III new cracks open parallel to the load. They open sideways, so lateral strain grows faster than in the elastic stage, and the sample starts to dilate relative to elastic behaviour. Growth is stable: if the load is held constant, the cracks stop.

At σcd cracks begin to interact and coalesce. Volumetric strain reverses: the sample, which had been compacting, starts to increase in volume. Crack growth is now unstable and can continue under constant load. Stage V follows the peak, where macroscopic fractures form and the sample loses strength rapidly.

Supplementary In practice these thresholds tell you where damage starts. Laboratory reports that include lateral strain gauges let you determine σci and σcd as well as the UCS. Eberhardt et al. (1998) found these thresholds were essentially independent of loading conditions, unlike peak strength.

Underground, a wall stressed above σci but below the UCS is accumulating damage. A long-term sustained load above σcd is unstable even if it is below the short-term peak.

εv = εaxial + 2 εlateral

Compression is positive and lateral strain is negative (extension). In the elastic stage, εlateral ≈ −ν εaxial, so volumetric strain increases linearly. Crack initiation is identified where lateral strain departs from linearity (Brace et al., 1966, as cited by Eberhardt et al., 1998). Crack damage is identified at the reversal point of the volumetric strain curve.

Facilitator notes Supplementary

Run the animation at 0.5× speed and stop at each threshold. Ask participants to predict what the volumetric strain curve will do next before resuming.

Section 1 · Intact rock · 1.3

Sample size and shape effects

Module 1 · slide 6

Key concept

UCS is not an intrinsic material property. It depends on sample size, shape and test conditions.

Why it matters

A strength measured on 50 mm core cannot be applied directly to a pillar or a stope wall that is metres across.

You should understand

The statistical size effect, the effect of slenderness, and the end effect created by steel platens.

Two samples of the same rock can give different UCS values just because they have different dimensions. The module separates a size effect (larger volume) and a shape effect (aspect ratio, length/diameter). Switch between them in the figure.

  • Size effect: brittleness increases with sample size. The module figure is labelled “size increases, strength decreases”.
  • Statistical effect: larger samples contain more micro-cracks, so they are more likely to include larger cracks or cracks oriented favourably for failure.
  • Young’s modulus is fairly unaffected by size.
  • Shape effect: peak strength and ductility decrease as the aspect ratio (slenderness) increases.
  • End effect: steel platens restrain the lateral expansion of the rock ends and create a triaxial compressive stress state there.

Failure starts at the weakest flaw. Think of the sample as a collection of small volumes, each with its own flaws. The more volume you test, the higher the chance that a critical flaw is present, so the measured strength falls. This is the “weakest link” idea behind the statistical size effect.

For shape, friction between the platens and the sample confines the ends. In a short, squat sample these confined zones overlap through most of the length, so the sample appears stronger and more ductile. In a slender sample the middle is free of end restraint and fails at a lower stress.

Supplementary This is why laboratory UCS is a starting point for rock mass strength, not the answer. Section 3 shows how the Hoek–Brown criterion with GSI reduces intact strength to rock mass strength. The shape effect also appears at field scale in pillars, where width-to-height ratio strongly affects pillar strength. Treat that as the same mechanism, confinement by the end surfaces, acting at a larger scale.

Supplementary Standard test methods specify sample slenderness and end preparation to limit these effects. The ISRM suggested method for uniaxial compression (Bieniawski and Bernede, 1979) calls for a length-to-diameter ratio of 2.5–3.0 and a diameter of at least 54 mm (NX), or 10 times the largest grain. Results from non-standard samples should be corrected or reported separately.

Review note on the slide text

The slide text reads “Peak strength and brittleness increase with sample size”, while the figure beside it, from Hudson and Harrison (1997), is labelled “Size increases, strength decreases”. The statistical explanation on the same slide (larger samples are more likely to contain critical flaws) supports a decrease in strength with size. This webinar follows the figure and the statistical explanation, and flags the slide wording for subject-matter review.

Facilitator notes Supplementary

Ask: “Your lab reports UCS = 150 MPa on NQ core. Would you expect a 10 m wide pillar of the same rock to carry 150 MPa average stress? Why not?” Collect the reasons: size, discontinuities, shape, confinement.

Section 1 · Intact rock · 1.4

Direct and indirect tension

Module 1 · slide 7

Key concept

Tensile strength is usually measured indirectly, by inducing tension through compressive loading.

Why it matters

Rock is much weaker in tension than in compression, and tensile cracking drives spalling and axial splitting.

You should understand

Why direct tension tests are rare, how the Brazilian test works, and why tensile strength depends on the test type.

A direct tension test pulls a cylinder apart along its axis. It is not commonly used. Instead, indirect tension tests load the sample in compression in a way that creates tensile stress inside it. The Brazilian test and the point load test are the module’s examples.

  • Direct tension is difficult in practice: it needs servo-controlled equipment with axial displacement as feedback, because rock in tension is ultra-brittle.
  • Rock does not fail in tension in situ (the module’s point): rock in the ground is loaded in compression, so a test that pulls the rock apart does not represent field loading.
  • Indirect tests induce tensile stress by compressive loading (Brazilian disc, point load).
  • The tensile stress, and therefore the strength, is calculated with linear elasticity from the load and the specimen dimensions.
  • Tensile strength is not an intrinsic property: it varies with test type.

In the Brazilian test a disc is squeezed across its diameter. Elastic theory shows that, along most of the loaded diameter, the stress perpendicular to that diameter is tensile and nearly uniform. The disc therefore splits along the loaded diameter. Near the loading points the stresses are compressive and high, which is why the module’s figure (Li and Wong, 2013) shows crushed or sheared zones at the platens. Use the figure’s layer toggles to show the tensile zone and the contact zones.

Supplementary Brazilian tests are quick and use the same core as UCS tests, so they are common in site investigations. Point load testing is often done in core sheds and field laboratories because it needs minimal sample preparation. Treat values from different tests as different numbers: do not average a Brazilian strength with a direct tensile strength without stating it.

Tension also appears around openings. Section 5 shows that, for a circular opening, the crown boundary goes into tension when the horizontal stress is less than one third of the vertical stress (elastic theory).

Supplementary ISRM (1978) gives the Brazilian tensile strength as

σt = 2P / (π D t) ≈ 0.636 P / (D t)

where P is the load at failure, D the disc diameter and t the disc thickness. The suggested method recommends a diameter preferably not less than NX core size (54 mm) and a thickness-to-diameter ratio of about 0.5–0.6. The formula assumes the disc splits from the centre; if failure starts at the loading points, the result is not a valid tensile strength.

Facilitator notes Supplementary

Show the calculator, then ask: “If two labs report tensile strength from Brazilian and direct tests on the same rock, should the numbers match?” The module’s answer: no, tensile strength varies with test type.

Section 1 · Intact rock · 1.5

Effect of confining pressure

Module 1 · slides 8–9

Key concept

Confinement raises peak strength and post-peak ductility, and changes the failure mode from axial splitting to shear to distributed ductile shearing.

Why it matters

Rock at an excavation wall has almost no confinement; rock a few metres in has much more. The same rock behaves differently in each place.

You should understand

The brittle–ductile transition, why it depends on rock type, and the physical process behind each type of curve.

In a the core is surrounded by fluid pressure σ3 (the confining pressure) and loaded axially. As σ3 increases, the peak of the differential stress σ1 − σ3 rises, and the curve after the peak changes from a sharp drop to a long plateau and eventually to continued hardening. Change the confining pressure and the rock type to see both effects.

  • Peak strength and post-peak ductility increase with confinement.
  • The marks the change from strain softening (brittle) to strain hardening (ductile).
  • The transition depends on rock type: about 0 MPa for rock salt, below 10 MPa for chalk, 20–100 MPa for limestone, above 100 MPa for sandstone and well above 100 MPa for granite (Goodman, 1989).
  • Unconfined: elastic until abrupt failure, mostly tensile fractures sub-parallel to the load (axial splitting).
  • Moderately confined: axial fracturing is suppressed and a well-defined shear fracture forms.
  • Highly confined: a network of small shear fractures develops and the rock behaves in a ductile manner.

Axial splitting cracks open in the direction of the minimum principal stress. With no confinement, nothing resists that opening. As σ3 rises, it clamps those cracks shut, so the rock can only fail by sliding on an inclined shear plane, which needs more stress. At high confinement, friction on every small fracture is so large that slip is spread over many small fractures and the sample deforms without losing strength.

The module summarises the consequence for design: the failure mechanism changes from fracturing perpendicular to the minimum principal stress at no or low confinement, to shearing at higher confinement.

Supplementary At the boundary of an opening the stress normal to the free surface is zero, so the wall rock is effectively unconfined. Confinement increases with distance into the wall. Brittle rock at depth therefore tends to fail at the boundary by fractures parallel to the excavation surface, while the confined core of a pillar can carry a much higher stress than its skin. This is a direct application of the module’s statement that failure mode depends on confinement.

The module’s numerical example (Lisjak et al., 2014) shows simulated fracture patterns in Opalinus Clay at σ3 = 0, 0.5, 1, 5, 10 and 15 MPa. The patterns move from axial splitting through inclined shear bands to distributed conjugate shearing, the same progression seen in the laboratory. This is the type of analysis that later modules perform with FDEM.

In the Carrara marble data (Jaeger et al., 2007) the curves for 0 and 23.5 MPa end in failure (marked ×), while those at 165 and 326 MPa keep hardening.

Facilitator notes Supplementary

Select granite and move σ3 from 0 to 200 MPa. Ask: “At what depth in your mine could confinement ever reach 100 MPa?” Most mining stresses are far below the transition for hard rocks, so brittle behaviour dominates near openings.

Failure criteria and the Mohr–Coulomb criterion

Module 1 · slides 10–11

Key concept

A failure criterion expresses strength as a function of the principal stresses. Mohr–Coulomb says rock fails at a critical combination of shear and normal stress on a plane.

Why it matters

Mohr–Coulomb is the simplest and most widely used criterion. Its parameters c and φ appear in almost every geotechnical software package.

You should understand

How the Mohr circle relates to the envelope, the failure plane angle β = 45° + φ/2, the tensile cut-off, and where the criterion fits best.

In general, Strength = f(σ1, σ2, σ3). The two criteria most used in practice are Mohr–Coulomb, which relates shear and normal stress at failure, and Hoek–Brown, an empirical best fit to strength data in σ1–σ3 space (next screen).

In the figure, the stress state is drawn as a . Failure occurs when the circle touches the straight Mohr–Coulomb envelope.

  • |τ| = τ0 + μ σn, with τ0 = c the and μ = tan φ the coefficient of friction. c and μ are treated as two constants for the rock.
  • On a plane at angle β, the stresses are τ = ½(σ1−σ3) sin 2β and σn = ½(σ1+σ3) + ½(σ1−σ3) cos 2β.
  • At failure, 2β = 90° + φ, so the failure plane is at β = 45° + φ/2.
  • A tensile cut-off T0 is usually added to give a realistic uniaxial tensile strength.

Every plane through a point carries a different pair (σn, τ). All of those pairs lie on the Mohr circle, which has centre ½(σ1+σ3) and radius ½(σ1−σ3). Increasing σ1 enlarges the circle; increasing σ3 moves it to the right, away from the envelope. Failure happens on the plane whose point first touches the envelope. Drag the plane angle in the figure and watch which plane is critical.

Because the envelope is straight, it extends into tension unrealistically. The tensile cut-off truncates it so that the predicted tensile strength is reasonable.

The module states that Mohr–Coulomb is most suitable at high confining pressures, where shear failure dominates. At low confinement, failure occurs mostly as coalescence of tensile micro-cracks, which a linear shear criterion does not represent well.

Supplementary This matters near excavation walls, where confinement is low. A Mohr–Coulomb model calibrated to triaxial tests at high σ3 may overestimate the strength of the unconfined wall rock.

Supplementary In principal stress form, the same criterion is

σ1 = σc + k σ3, k = (1 + sin φ)/(1 − sin φ), σc = 2c cos φ/(1 − sin φ)

This is a straight line in σ1–σ3 space with intercept σc (the predicted UCS) and slope k. The figure uses it to find σ1 at failure for the chosen σ3.

Facilitator notes Supplementary

Switch to “Check a stress state” and keep σ1 fixed. Lower σ3 step by step. Ask: “What does removing confinement do, and where around an opening does that happen?”

Hoek–Brown criterion for intact rock

Module 1 · slide 12

Key concept

Hoek–Brown is an empirical, curved envelope fitted to triaxial strength data in σ1–σ3 space.

Why it matters

The curvature matches real rock data better than a straight line, especially at low confinement where mining openings are.

You should understand

The roles of σci and mi, and how the curve compares with a Mohr–Coulomb line.

σ′1 = σ′3 + σci ( mi σ′3/σci + 1 )0.5

The criterion is plotted in normalised form, σ1/σci against σ3/σci, as in the module’s figures. Change mi to see how the envelope steepens.

  • σci: unconfined compressive strength of the intact rock.
  • mi: a parameter related to the degree of particle interlocking in the rock, which depends on rock type and strength.
  • The module shows experimental data for granites with different σci fitted by one value of m = 27.9 (r² = 0.99), and envelopes for m = 10 and m = 5.
  • Inter-criteria relations with Mohr–Coulomb are available.

At σ3 = 0 the equation gives σ1 = σci, so the curve passes through the UCS. As σ3 increases, the square root makes the strength gain per unit of confinement fall off. A higher mi means a steeper rise at low confinement: well-interlocked crystalline rocks gain strength quickly when confined.

The curve also extends into negative σ3 and meets the axis at a tensile strength that depends on mi.

Supplementary A Mohr–Coulomb line fitted to Hoek–Brown over one stress range is only valid in that range. Fit it over the confinements your problem will experience. For a shallow drift wall that is a low σ3 range, for a deep pillar core a higher one. The toggle in the figure draws a Mohr–Coulomb line through two chosen points of the Hoek–Brown curve so you can see where the two disagree.

Supplementary Setting σ1 = 0 in the intact Hoek–Brown equation and solving gives the predicted biaxial tensile strength:

σt / σci = ( mi − √(mi² + 4) ) / 2

For mi = 27.9 this is about −0.036, a tensile strength of roughly σci/28. This value follows from the equation; it is not a measured tensile strength.

Facilitator notes Supplementary

Compare m = 27.9 and m = 5 at σ3/σci = 0.2. Ask which rock benefits more from rock bolts or other support that adds confinement at the wall.

Recap and knowledge check

Recap

  • The uniaxial stress–strain curve has concave-up, linear (E), concave-down, peak (UCS) and post-peak parts. Cracking starts at about 0.4 UCS.
  • Brittle failure runs through five stages: crack closure, linear elastic, stable crack growth (σci), unstable growth with dilation (σcd), and failure.
  • UCS and tensile strength are not intrinsic properties: they depend on size, shape and test type.
  • Confinement increases strength and ductility and changes the failure mode from axial splitting to shear.
  • Mohr–Coulomb is linear in shear and normal stress and fits best at high confinement; Hoek–Brown is a curved empirical fit in σ1–σ3 space.

Scenario: two sets of lab results

A consultant sends UCS results for the same sandstone unit from two campaigns. Campaign A used 54 mm core with length/diameter = 2.5; campaign B used 85 mm core trimmed to length/diameter = 1.0. Campaign B reports higher strengths. The mine planner wants to use the higher values for stope pillar design.

Work through: Which effects from this section could explain the difference? Which campaign is more representative of the standard test, and what would you ask the consultant before using either value for pillar design?

Discussion prompts

  1. In which places in your mine is rock close to unconfined (σ3 ≈ 0)? What failure mode would you expect there?
  2. Do your laboratory reports include lateral strain or acoustic emission? What extra information would σci and σcd give you?

Section 2 · Discontinuities · 2.1

Geometrical properties of discontinuities

Module 1 · slide 14

Key concept

A is any separation in the rock continuum with effectively zero tensile strength.

Why it matters

Discontinuities control the strength, deformability and permeability of the rock mass. Their presence is what makes rock mechanics a unique subject.

You should understand

The primary geometrical properties, spacing, orientation and persistence, and how each is measured.

Joints, bedding planes, faults, foliation and other breaks in the rock are all grouped under the word discontinuity. The schematic shows a rock exposure with a borehole or scanline crossing it. Each label is one property used to describe the discontinuities. Select a label to see its definition and how it affects rock behaviour.

  • Spacing: distance between adjacent discontinuity intersections with the measuring scanline.
  • Orientation: dip direction and dip angle.
  • Persistence, size and shape: extent of the discontinuity in its own plane, linked to the presence of .
  • The schematic also shows the other descriptors in Hudson’s figure: roughness, aperture, filling, seepage, wall strength, discontinuity sets and block size. Roughness, aperture, sets and blocks are covered on the next screen.

Because a discontinuity carries effectively no tension, rock on either side can separate or slide. Close spacing means smaller blocks and a weaker, more deformable mass. Orientation decides whether a discontinuity is loaded mainly in shear or in compression by a given stress or excavation face. Persistence decides whether the discontinuity can form a complete release surface or whether intact rock bridges must break first.

Supplementary Scanline mapping on drift walls and bench faces, and logging of oriented core, are the usual ways these properties are measured at mines. The suggested methods of the ISRM (1978) for the quantitative description of discontinuities list ten parameters: orientation, spacing, persistence, roughness, wall strength, aperture, filling, seepage, number of sets and block size. These are the same labels shown in the schematic.

Supplementary A scanline that is not perpendicular to a set records an apparent spacing larger than the true (normal) spacing:

apparent spacing = true spacing / cos θ

where θ is the angle between the scanline and the normal to the set. A scanline nearly parallel to a set will barely sample it. This orientation bias is why several scanlines in different directions are mapped. The lower tool in the figure demonstrates it.

Facilitator notes Supplementary

Rotate the scanline until it is almost parallel to the joints. Ask: “If all your mapping is on east–west drives, which joint sets will you under-record?”

Section 2 · Discontinuities · 2.2

Roughness, aperture, sets and rock blocks

Module 1 · slide 15

Key concept

Surface roughness and aperture describe a single discontinuity; sets and blocks describe how discontinuities combine.

Why it matters

Roughness controls shear strength. Sets and their spacing control the size and shape of blocks that can fall or slide.

You should understand

How JRC is assigned, what aperture means for rough surfaces, and how block shape follows from set geometry.

The upper panel shows roughness and aperture on one discontinuity. The lower panel shows how two or three sets of discontinuities cut the rock into blocks. Change the number of sets, their spacing and orientation, and see the block shape change.

  • Roughness: defined by reference to a standard chart, such as Barton’s , or mathematically. JRC runs from 0 (smooth, planar) to 20 (very rough).
  • Aperture: the perpendicular distance between the adjacent rock surfaces. It varies along rough surfaces.
  • Discontinuity sets: because of how they form, discontinuities cluster around preferred orientations.
  • Rock blocks form as a function of the geometrical properties of the sets.

A rough surface must ride up over its asperities (or break them) to slide, so it is stronger in shear and dilates as it moves. Two rough surfaces touch only at some points, so aperture is not a single number but a distribution, with contact points where the walls meet. Fluid flows through the open parts, which is why aperture controls permeability.

Blocks are bounded by surfaces from different sets. One set alone gives slabs; two sets give columns or prisms; three sets give closed blocks whose size depends on the spacings and whose shape depends on the angles between sets.

Supplementary In an underground opening, blocks formed by three or more sets can be released by the excavation and fall or slide from the back or walls if they are not supported. Recording sets, spacings and orientations during mapping lets an engineer check which blocks the excavation geometry can release. In open pits the same data feed bench-scale kinematic checks.

JRC is used directly in the Barton–Bandis shear strength criterion (screen 2.4). The module reproduces ten typical profiles at reduced scale (after Barton and Choubey), from JRC 0–2 up to 18–20. Matching a measured profile to the chart is subjective; mathematical definitions based on profile statistics are an alternative.

Facilitator notes Supplementary

Set three sets with one set widely spaced. Ask what block shape results and how it would behave in a drift back.

Section 2 · Discontinuities · 2.3

Mechanical properties: stiffness

Module 1 · slide 16

Key concept

A discontinuity has a stress–displacement response in compression, tension and shear. Its stiffness is stress per unit displacement.

Why it matters

Joint normal and shear stiffness are standard inputs to discontinuum and FDEM models, and they control how much a jointed mass deforms.

You should understand

Why normal stiffness increases with load, why tension is zero, and how shear behaviour resembles an intact-rock curve.

Load the same discontinuity in three ways and plot stress against displacement across or along it. Use the tabs in the figure to switch between compression, tension and shear, then animate the loading.

Stiffness has units of stress per length, for example MPa/m, because it relates a stress to a displacement rather than to a strain.

  • Compression: normal stiffness increases with load, approaching a threshold value associated with the intact rock strength.
  • Tension: no tensile stress can be sustained, by definition.
  • Shear: similar to the intact-rock compression curve: initial shear stiffness and linear response, peak strength, then post-peak behaviour.
  • Mathematical models, for example hyperbolic ones, relate normal stress to normal displacement and shear stress to shear displacement.

Under normal load, the contacting asperities are few at first, so the joint closes easily. As closure proceeds, more asperities come into contact and the contact area grows, so each additional increment of stress produces less closure. The joint cannot close by more than its initial aperture, so the curve becomes nearly vertical, approaching the stiffness of the intact rock.

In shear, the joint first deforms elastically, then asperities ride over or shear through each other until the peak shear strength is reached. Beyond the peak, damaged asperities provide less resistance and the stress falls towards a residual value.

Supplementary In numerical models of jointed rock, joint normal stiffness kn and shear stiffness ks control the deformation of the mass before any slip. Values from small laboratory samples are scale-dependent. They are usually adjusted by calibration or back-analysis of monitored deformations such as extensometer readings.

The normal stiffness is the tangent slope of the normal stress–closure curve:

kn = dσn / dδn, ks = dτ / dδs

Supplementary A common hyperbolic closure model (Bandis et al., 1983) is σn = kni δn / (1 − δn/Vm), where kni is the initial normal stiffness and Vm the maximum closure. The compression panel uses this form with relative values.

Section 2 · Discontinuities · 2.4

Mechanical properties: shear strength

Module 1 · slide 17

Key concept

Discontinuity shear strength is expressed with Mohr–Coulomb (c, φ) or with the non-linear Barton–Bandis criterion based on JRC and JCS.

Why it matters

Wedge, planar and step-path stability checks all depend on joint shear strength at the normal stresses acting in the field.

You should understand

Why roughness matters more at low normal stress, and what instantaneous cohesion and friction angle mean.

Mohr–Coulomb: τp = c + σn tan φ

Barton–Bandis: τ = σn tan[ JRC log10(JCS/σn) + φr ]

The figure plots both. Move the normal stress marker to see the tangent to the Barton–Bandis curve, which defines the instantaneous cohesion and friction angle at that stress.

  • JRC: Joint Roughness Coefficient, the geometrical component of roughness.
  • JCS: Joint wall Compressive Strength. JCS/σn is the asperity failure component.
  • φr: residual friction angle of the surface.
  • Instantaneous cohesion ci and friction angle φi: the intercept and slope of the tangent at a given σn (Hoek, 2006).

The criterion accounts for the reduction of effective roughness as asperities are damaged during shearing. At low normal stress, JCS/σn is large, the logarithm is large, and roughness adds many degrees to the friction angle: the asperities ride over each other. At high normal stress, JCS/σn approaches 1, the logarithm approaches zero, and the asperities are sheared off, so strength tends towards residual friction.

That is why the curve bends: the effective friction angle falls as normal stress rises.

Supplementary Joints in the back of a shallow drift or on a bench face carry low normal stress, which is the range where Barton–Bandis gives the largest roughness contribution and where a single linear c–φ fit can be misleading. If a limit-equilibrium program needs c and φ, take the instantaneous values at the normal stress expected on the joint, not at a laboratory stress level.

Instantaneous friction angle and cohesion at σn:

φi = arctan(dτ/dσn), ci = τ − σn tan φi

Supplementary The logarithm term must not be negative, so the criterion is used only for σn ≤ JCS (Barton and Choubey, 1977, as summarised in the GeoStudio rock joint strength models document). The figure stops the curve at σn = JCS.

Section 3 · Rock mass · 3.1

Strength of rock masses: the plane of weakness

Module 1 · slide 19

Key concept

A sample containing one discontinuity has a strength that depends on the angle between the discontinuity and the principal stresses.

Why it matters

It shows in the simplest possible case how structure makes rock strength directional, and how several sets make it more uniform again.

You should understand

When failure goes through intact rock and when it slides on the discontinuity, and why many sets tend towards isotropic strength.

Mathematical expressions show how rock strength is affected by discontinuities. The simplest case, an intact sample with one discontinuity, is analysed with the single plane of weakness theory. Rotate the discontinuity and watch the strength σ1 − σ3 change. Then add more sets.

  • The strength depends on the relative orientation of the discontinuity and the principal stresses.
  • The failure mode changes from failure of the stronger intact rock to shearing of the weaker discontinuity.
  • With several discontinuities, each weakens the rock in a different direction at the same time, and the rock mass tends to become isotropic in strength.
  • The real situation is further complicated because stress is not transmitted directly across discontinuities.

When the discontinuity is nearly parallel or nearly perpendicular to σ1, the shear stress on it is small compared with the normal stress, so it cannot slip and the sample fails through intact rock. In between, the shear stress on the plane is large enough to overcome its friction and cohesion, and the sample slides along it at a lower stress. The minimum strength occurs at βw = 45° + φw/2, the same angle as the Mohr–Coulomb failure plane, but using the joint’s friction angle.

With two or more sets at different angles, whichever set is most unfavourably oriented controls. The troughs of the curves overlap and the minimum strength becomes nearly the same in every direction.

Supplementary A foliated or bedded host rock with one dominant weak set behaves like this: pillars or walls whose geometry loads that set in shear at a critical angle can fail at stresses far below the intact strength. The direction of the drive or the orientation of stope walls relative to the set and to the principal stresses then matters a great deal.

Supplementary The single plane of weakness relation (Jaeger’s theory, given in Hudson and Harrison, 1997, and Jaeger et al., 2007):

σ1 − σ3 = 2(cw + σ3 tan φw) / [ (1 − tan φw cot βw) sin 2βw ]

βw is the angle between σ1 and the normal to the discontinuity. Slip is only possible for φw < βw < 90°; outside that range the intact strength (here Mohr–Coulomb) governs.

Section 3 · Rock mass · 3.2

Generalized Hoek–Brown failure criterion

Module 1 · slide 20Supplementary: parameter equations (Hoek et al., 2002)

Key concept

The Generalized Hoek–Brown criterion reduces intact strength to rock mass strength using GSI and the disturbance factor D.

Why it matters

It is the most common way to estimate jointed rock mass strength for mine design and numerical models.

You should understand

The three properties needed (σci, mi, GSI), and how mb, s and a follow from them.

σ′1 = σ′3 + σci ( mb σ′3/σci + s )a

The generalized form applies to jointed rock masses. For intact rock, mb = mi, s = 1 and a = 0.5, and it reduces to the equation on screen 1.7. Lower the GSI to see the envelope fall below the intact curve.

  • σci: unconfined compressive strength of the intact rock.
  • mb: Hoek–Brown constant for the rock mass.
  • s and a: constants that depend on rock mass characteristics.
  • Three rock mass properties must be estimated: σci, the intact constant mi, and the , a number that estimates the reduction of intact strength as a function of geological conditions.
  • Empirical relations give mb, s and a from σci, mi, GSI and the .

GSI describes how blocky the rock mass is and how good its discontinuity surfaces are. GSI = 100 corresponds to intact rock; lower values mean smaller, less interlocked blocks and weaker surfaces. As GSI falls, s drops sharply (s = 0 means no cohesive strength at σ3 = 0), mb falls, and a rises slightly above 0.5. The rock mass UCS, σci sa, can be a small fraction of the intact UCS.

D accounts for damage from blasting and stress relaxation. It varies from 0 for undisturbed in-situ rock masses to 1 for very disturbed rock masses.

Supplementary At a mine, GSI is usually estimated from face or core mapping using the GSI chart, and σci and mi from triaxial testing or published tables. Hoek et al. (2002) give D examples: D = 0 for excellent quality controlled blasting or TBM excavation, and D = 1.0 for heavy production blasting in large open pit slopes. D applies only to the damaged zone, not to the whole rock mass.

Supplementary Hoek, Carranza-Torres and Corkum (2002):

mb = mi exp[(GSI − 100)/(28 − 14D)]

s = exp[(GSI − 100)/(9 − 3D)]

a = ½ + ⅙ (e−GSI/15 − e−20/3)

σc,mass = σci sa, σt ≈ −s σci/mb

Section 3 · Rock mass · 3.3

When the Hoek–Brown criterion applies

Module 1 · slide 21

Key concept

Hoek–Brown assumes isotropic rock and rock mass behaviour, an “equivalent medium”.

Why it matters

Applying it where one structure controls behaviour can give a stability answer that is badly wrong.

You should understand

The scale conditions for using GSI and Hoek–Brown, and when discontinuities must be modelled explicitly.

The same excavation can sit in intact rock, in rock with one joint, in sparsely jointed rock, in blocky rock or in heavily jointed rock. As the number of discontinuities around it increases relative to its size, the rock mass behaves more and more like a uniform, isotropic material. Select each case to see the module’s guidance.

The criterion is applicable only when:

  • there are a sufficient number of closely spaced discontinuities with similar surface characteristics (the Hoek–Brown equivalent medium);
  • the structure being analysed is large and the block size is small in comparison.

It is not applicable when:

  • the structure size is of the same order as the block size;
  • one discontinuity set is significantly weaker than the others.

Hoek–Brown smears the effect of discontinuities into a continuous, direction-independent strength. That works when the excavation “sees” many small blocks, so no single joint dominates. When blocks are large compared with the opening, failure follows specific discontinuities: a wedge slides, a slab buckles. A smeared continuum cannot represent those mechanisms. When one set is much weaker, strength is strongly directional (screen 3.1), which an isotropic criterion cannot capture.

Supplementary A large open pit slope in heavily jointed rock is a typical Hoek–Brown case. A 4–5 m drift in blocky rock with joints spaced at 1–2 m is not: its stability is controlled by individual wedges, so a structural or discontinuum analysis is needed. A weak, persistent fault or shear zone crossing a stope should be modelled explicitly even if the surrounding rock mass suits Hoek–Brown.

The module’s figure gives the guidance for each case (Hoek and Brown, 2018):

  • Intact rock: do not use GSI; use Hoek–Brown to check for tensile and shear failure.
  • Single joint: do not use GSI; model the joint explicitly and use Hoek–Brown for intact rock.
  • Sparsely jointed rock: do not use GSI; model joints explicitly and use Hoek–Brown for intact rock.
  • Blocky rock mass with minimal anisotropy: use GSI with caution.
  • Heavily jointed rock mass: use of GSI is appropriate.

Section 3 · Rock mass · 3.4

Anisotropy and heterogeneity

Module 1 · slide 22

Key concept

: properties vary with direction. : properties vary with location.

Why it matters

Most closed-form solutions and conventional models assume a CHILE rock mass, while real rock is DIANE.

You should understand

The meaning of CHILE and DIANE, the scales at which variability appears, and what simplifying assumptions cost.

Engineers simplify rock in order to calculate. The simplified rock is CHILE: continuous, homogeneous, isotropic, linear elastic. Real rock is DIANE: discontinuous, inhomogeneous, anisotropic, non-elastic. Switch each property in the figure to see what the simplification removes.

  • Examples of properties that vary: deformability modulus, strength, permeability, discontinuity frequency.
  • Scales: microscale (grains, crystals, microstructure), laboratory scale (core samples of intact rock), engineering scale (volume of rock mass).
  • CHILE: the assumption traditionally used in practice, often needed for closed-form solutions, and the basis of conventional numerical modelling.
  • DIANE: real-world conditions. Advanced modelling techniques can deal with at least some of these aspects.

Anisotropy usually comes from an ordered fabric: bedding, foliation, schistosity or a dominant joint set. The UCS of a schist, for example, changes with the angle between the load and the schistosity, with a minimum at an intermediate angle (module figure after Hoek, 2006), for the same reason as the plane of weakness on screen 3.1.

Heterogeneity comes from different lithologies, alteration, veining or weathering. What looks homogeneous at one scale can be heterogeneous at another.

Supplementary Ore zones are often more altered, veined or foliated than the host rock, so the hanging wall, ore and footwall may need different properties and sometimes directional ones. A model that is CHILE everywhere is a deliberate simplification. Record which DIANE aspects it ignores and judge whether they could control the result.

Linear elastic anisotropy is handled by the stiffness or compliance matrix (Section 4). Discontinuity and non-elastic behaviour need explicit joints, plasticity or fracture models, such as the FDEM approach used later in the course.

Section 4 · Constitutive models · 4.1

Constitutive models: definition

Module 1 · slide 24

Key concept

A is a phenomenological law describing the stress–strain behaviour of rock treated as a continuum.

Why it matters

Every continuum numerical model of a mine needs one. The choice decides what behaviour the model can reproduce.

You should understand

The general form f(σ, ε, σ̇, ε̇) = 0, the matrix forms σ = Sε and ε = Cσ, and the trade-off between complexity and data.

“Phenomenological” means the law describes observed behaviour, as in the curves of Section 1, rather than modelling every crack and grain. A constitutive model is the rule a numerical model uses to turn strains into stresses at each point.

f(σ, ε, σ̇, ε̇) = 0

  • σ: stress. ε: strain. A dot means the rate with respect to time.
  • If stresses and strains do not change with time under constant load (time dependency neglected), the model can be written in matrix form.
  • σ = Sε: stress–strain form, with S the stiffness matrix.
  • ε = Cσ: strain–stress (inverse) form, with C the compliance matrix.

S and C are inverses of each other (C = S−1). In three dimensions each is a 6 × 6 matrix linking the six independent stress components to the six strain components. Including the rate terms σ̇ and ε̇ allows time-dependent behaviour such as creep; dropping them gives the rate-independent models used in most mining analyses.

The module’s key point: as the complexity of the constitutive model increases, so does the number of input parameters required and, consequently, the amount of laboratory data. Supplementary A sophisticated model fed with guessed parameters is not more reliable than a simple one with well-supported parameters. Choose the simplest model that can show the mechanism you are worried about.

In Voigt notation, σ = [σxx, σyy, σzz, τyz, τzx, τxy]T and ε uses the engineering shear strains γ. The number of independent entries in S or C depends on material symmetry, as the next screen shows.

Section 4 · Constitutive models · 4.2

Linear-elastic constitutive models

Module 1 · slide 25

Key concept

Linear elasticity assumes a reversible, linear stress–strain relationship (Hooke’s law).

Why it matters

Elastic models are used for stress analysis around openings and as the pre-failure part of most other models.

You should understand

How the number of elastic constants depends on the symmetry of the material: 21, 9, 5 or 2.

The compliance matrix C has 36 entries but is symmetric, so at most 21 are independent. Each symmetry in the rock removes some of them. Select a symmetry class to see which entries remain independent.

  • General anisotropic rock: 21 elastic constants (the matrix is symmetric, so 21 rather than 36).
  • Orthotropic rock: three axes of symmetry, for example a rock mass with three orthogonal fracture sets. 9 constants: 3 Young’s moduli, 3 Poisson’s ratios and 3 shear moduli.
  • Transversely isotropic rock: one axis of symmetry, for example rock with distinct laminations or one main fracture set. 5 constants: 2 Young’s moduli, 2 Poisson’s ratios, 1 shear modulus.
  • Perfectly isotropic rock: 2 constants, Young’s modulus and Poisson’s ratio.

“Linear” means that doubling the stress doubles the strain. “Elastic” means that removing the load returns the rock to its original shape with no permanent strain. Symmetry means that some directions are equivalent: in an isotropic rock all directions are, so one E and one ν describe everything and the shear modulus follows as G = E/[2(1 + ν)].

Supplementary A laminated or foliated rock (shale, schist, banded iron formation, layered ore) is often modelled as transversely isotropic, with the stiffer direction parallel to the layering. Using an isotropic model there can misplace stress concentrations around an opening.

For the transversely isotropic case on slide 25, E and ν apply in the plane of isotropy, E′ and ν′ across it, and G′ is the shear modulus for planes normal to it. The in-plane shear modulus follows from E and ν, so it is not an independent constant: 2(1 + ν)/E appears in the matrix.

Section 4 · Constitutive models · 4.3

Elasto-plastic constitutive models

Module 1 · slide 26

Key concept

In an elasto-plastic model, loading beyond the yield point produces irreversible (plastic) strain, so stress and strain no longer correspond one-to-one.

Why it matters

These models are used to analyse progressive failure of rock masses, for example yielding pillars and failed zones around openings.

You should understand

Loading and unloading paths, and the three post-peak models and the rock mass qualities they suit.

With elasto-plastic models there is no unique correspondence between stress and strain states: the current strain depends on the loading history. Animate the two loading paths in the figure, then compare the three post-peak models.

  • When load increases, the material behaves elastically until it reaches a stress state defined by a failure (yield) criterion. Unloaded before that, it regains its original state (line AB).
  • Strained beyond the yield point and then unloaded, it develops irreversible (plastic) deformation (lines BCF and BCD).
  • The model needs a failure criterion for the rock mass, for example Hoek–Brown, and a post-peak model: elastic–brittle, elastic–plastic with strain-softening, or elastic–perfectly plastic.

Unloading from point C follows a line parallel to the elastic loading line AB, not the original path. When the stress reaches zero at D, the strain that remains, εD, is the plastic strain. Reloading would follow D–C elastically and then continue yielding towards F.

The post-peak model defines what happens to strength once yielding starts: an immediate drop to residual (brittle), a gradual drop (strain-softening) or no drop at all (perfectly plastic).

The module gives the suggested post-peak models for different rock mass qualities (Hoek, 2006):

  • very good quality hard rock mass: elastic–brittle;
  • average quality rock mass: strain-softening;
  • very poor quality soft rock mass: elastic–perfectly plastic.

Supplementary Choosing perfectly plastic behaviour for a brittle hard rock will usually underestimate how quickly a failed zone loses strength.

In incremental form, total strain is split into elastic and plastic parts, dε = dεe + dεp. The elastic part follows Hooke’s law; the plastic part develops only while the stress state is on the yield surface. Supplementary A flow rule, often controlled by a dilation angle, decides the direction of plastic strain. That is an additional input parameter that the module’s complexity–data trade-off applies to.

Section 5 · In-situ stress · 5.1

In-situ stress: definition and importance

Module 1 · slide 28

Key concept

Rock is already stressed before excavation, by the weight of the overlying strata and by locked-in tectonic stresses.

Why it matters

The in-situ stress field is the single most important boundary condition for analysing underground structures.

You should understand

That the stress state at a point needs six parameters, and that excavation turns the in-situ state into an induced state.

Rock mechanics and rock engineering often deal with a pre-existing state of stress in the ground. It results from the weight of the overlying strata and from locked-in stresses of tectonic origin. This in-situ stress field has to be defined and understood for analysis and design. In the module this term is kept in English: in-situ stress.

  • At any point, the stress state is defined by the three principal stress magnitudes σ1 ≥ σ2 ≥ σ3 and their orientations: six parameters in total.
  • (a) principal stresses on a small cube; (b) the principal stress tensor, diagonal with σ1, σ2, σ3; (c) the three mutually perpendicular directions on a hemispherical projection.
  • Excavations and other loads disturb the in-situ state, producing an state.
  • The expected rock failure direction is strongly related to the in-situ stress field.

Why six? Three numbers give the magnitudes. Three angles orient the set of three perpendicular axes: for example the trend and plunge of σ1 plus one rotation about σ1 that fixes σ2 and σ3. Rotate the axes in the figure and watch all three points move together on the projection while staying 90° apart.

Gravity alone gives a vertical stress that increases with depth. Tectonic stresses are often horizontal and can make a horizontal stress the largest principal stress, so σ1 is not necessarily vertical.

Supplementary The vertical stress is commonly estimated as σv = γz. With a unit weight of about 0.027 MN/m³ this is about 0.027 MPa per metre of depth, roughly 1 MPa for every 37–40 m (Hoek and Brown, 1980, as presented in the UBC EOSC 433 lecture notes). Horizontal stresses are much less predictable and should be measured, for example by overcoring or hydraulic fracturing, before major design decisions.

The stress tensor in any coordinate system has six independent components (three normal, three shear). Rotating into the principal axes makes the shear components zero, leaving the diagonal form shown in (b). Six parameters are needed either way.

Supplementary On a lower-hemisphere equal-area projection, a direction with plunge p plots at radius R·√2·sin((90° − p)/2) from the centre, along its trend.

Section 5 · In-situ stress · 5.2

From in-situ to induced stress around an opening

Extends slide 28: “in-situ stress is disturbed by excavations”Kirsch solution (Kirsch, 1898; Brady and Brown, 2006)

Key concept

An excavation redistributes the in-situ stress. The boundary stress depends on the ratio of horizontal to vertical stress, K.

Why it matters

Where the induced boundary stress is highest is where stress-driven damage is expected; where it is tensile, loosening is possible.

You should understand

How changing K moves the stress concentration between crown and sidewalls, using the simplest CHILE case.

This screen uses the classic elastic solution for a circular hole in a CHILE material to show what the module means by an induced stress state. Set the depth and K and read the tangential stress around the boundary.

  • p: vertical in-situ stress (from depth). K p: horizontal in-situ stress in the plane of the section.
  • σθ: tangential (hoop) stress along the boundary.
  • σr: radial stress, which is zero on the boundary (free surface).
  • Crown (roof) and sidewall are the two extremes.

The opening removes rock that was carrying load, so stress flows around it. The tangential stress rises where the flow is squeezed (sidewalls when vertical stress dominates) and drops where it is relieved. For K = 1 the boundary stress is 2p everywhere. For K = 0 it is 3p at the sidewalls and −p (tension) in the crown.

Because σr = 0 at the wall, the boundary rock is in an unconfined state, the condition in which Section 1 showed axial splitting and spalling-type failure.

Supplementary With a high horizontal stress (K > 1), damage is expected in the crown and floor; with a low one (K < 1) in the sidewalls. This is why the orientation of a drive relative to the principal stresses matters, and why the module calls the in-situ stress the single most important boundary condition.

Supplementary Tangential stress on the boundary of a circular opening (θ measured from the horizontal axis):

σθ = p [ (1 + K) + 2(1 − K) cos 2θ ]

sidewall (θ = 0°): σθ = (3 − K) p crown (θ = 90°): σθ = (3K − 1) p

The crown goes into tension when K < 1/3. These follow from the Kirsch equations (Kirsch, 1898; given in Brady and Brown, 2006) for r = a.

Module 1 · whole module

The chain of ideas

  • Intact rock fails progressively. Strength depends on size, shape, test type and especially confinement.
  • Discontinuities have zero tensile strength, finite stiffness and a shear strength that depends on roughness and normal stress.
  • The rock mass combines both. Its strength can be directional (one weak set) or near-isotropic (many sets), and Hoek–Brown with GSI applies only to the second case.
  • Constitutive models encode this behaviour for numerical analysis; more realism needs more data.
  • The in-situ stress is the starting load. Excavation turns it into the induced stress that the rock must carry.

Limitations

  • The interactive figures are teaching models. Curves marked “illustrative” have the right shape and trend but no units or test data behind them. Calculators (Brazilian, Mohr–Coulomb, Barton–Bandis, Hoek–Brown, Kirsch) implement published equations with example inputs. None of them is a design tool, and none replaces site investigation, testing or review by a qualified engineer.
  • The module PDF has no speaker notes. The explanations here expand the slide text using the module’s own figures and the supplementary sources above.
  • No regulatory requirements, operating procedures or equipment specifications are given or implied.
  • No videos are embedded. The animations were built for this webinar so it works without an internet connection.

Items for subject-matter review

  • Slide 6: the text says peak strength increases with sample size; the figure on the same slide and the statistical explanation indicate it decreases. The webinar follows the figure.
  • Slide 29: Jaeger, Cook and Zimmerman is listed as 2017; the 4th edition is dated 2007. Slide 9 (figure credit) also cites Jaeger et al. (2007).
  • Slide 15: JRC profiles are credited to Barton and Choubey (1979); the paper usually cited for JRC is Barton and Choubey (1977).
  • Mining examples marked “Supplementary” are general illustrations. Check them against site conditions before using them in training at a specific operation.
  • The ISRM suggested-method details (Brazilian specimen size, UCS slenderness 2.5–3.0, point load index) were checked against secondary summaries, not the original papers. Confirm them against the current editions in the ISRM “Blue Book”.
